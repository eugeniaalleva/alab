# What is this study about?

The purpose of this study is to develop models to identify heavy menstrual bleeding that leads to iron deficiency, improve screening and reduce delays in diagnosis.

You will receive a **$25 Amazon gift card** after completing the study.

# Who can participate?

To be eligible to participate in this study, an individual must meet all of the following criteria:

- Have between 18 and 45 years of age

- Experience menstrual periods, with at least 1 menstruation in the last 90 days

- Make regular use of menstrual tracking applications on your smartphone

- Are not pregnant or have been pregnant in the last 9 months

- Never had both your ovaries or your uterus surgically removed

- Had a ferritin or hemoglobin blood test in the past 3 months at Mount Sinai

# How to enroll

To enroll in the study you will need to:

- Download our ehive app at this [link](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ehive-studies/id1536952138)

- Once signed into the app, click on the upper right corner (lock icon) and type the unique patient-linked code provided to you

- Answer the questions to check that you are eligible, and sign the e-consent

# What does participation involve?

During study participation you will:

- Complete short surveys

- Complete a menstrual diary for one full menstruation

- Authorize sharing of health information already collected on your smartphone (through Apple Health or Google Health) such as activity, sleep, and heart rate

- Provide permission to link your Mount Sinai health records, including lab tests, imaging, and—if available—pathology or genetic data from Mount Sinai research programs

Participation will take about 1–3 months, depending on the timing of your next menstrual cycle.

# Why is this important?

1 in 3 menstruating people experience heavy menstrual bleeding, and about half of them develop iron deficiency. This can significantly affect quality of life, leading to fatigue, brain fog, and increased absence from school or work. Yet, on average, it takes five years for patients to receive a diagnosis and effective treatment. This study aims to change that by developing a simple, rapid screening tool to shorten the time to diagnosis.
